# What "social good" means here

> **Status: working draft for the team, written 2026-10-24.** BRIDGEGOOD authors and owns the Design for Social Good philosophy. This document is our reading of it, built from `docs/challenge.md`, the team spec, and BRIDGEGOOD's public pages (apprenticeship, learn/how-it-connects, accelerator, tuition, design). Anything we propose here needs BRIDGEGOOD's review before it shows up as their language in the app.

The brief warns that "social good", "inclusion", "access" and "human-centered" can turn into branding. This doc gives each one a meaning you can see in what people do. It also says how you'd notice when it's missing, and what that means for the app.

The "signals" below help us design flows and talk with each other. They are **not** a scoring checklist to show users. The brief rules that out, and the philosophy depends on judgment and relationships that a list can't hold.

---

## 1. What makes BRIDGEGOOD's approach different

Most "design for social impact" follows one pattern: skilled professionals donate design work to a community that needs it. Human-centered design (HCD) adds a method: research users, then design around their needs. BRIDGEGOOD keeps the method and changes five other things.

### 1.1 The designers are beneficiaries too

In pro bono and CSR design, the community benefits and the designer gives. At BRIDGEGOOD, the people doing the design are themselves from communities the design industry overlooks: BIPOC designers, people on nontraditional paths, people with "no portfolio or network" (the founder's own starting point). A project is only "good" if **two things** happen. The community gets something that works for it, **and** the designers grow in confidence, access, community and economic mobility.

*Why this matters for the app:* every flow has two outcomes to care about. A capstone that ships a great site but leaves the student team without credit, relationships or a portfolio piece has failed half the test.

### 1.2 Access means removing material barriers, not only meeting WCAG

BRIDGEGOOD's public pages treat access as money, food, time, tools and networks, as well as disability:

- $0 tuition, no income share agreements, no premium tiers
- free professional software licences
- lunch and snacks, on the reasoning that hunger reduces focus and food stabilizes attendance
- one-to-one coaching and social-worker support for life challenges
- introductions to working professionals, because referrals carry so much weight in hiring

Sponsors fund this through cohorts and seats. Most generic "accessible design" stops at the interface.

### 1.3 Hard things are named, with a person there to hold the conversation

The brief lists conversations about identity, racism, discrimination and workplace challenges, with a **social-worker-informed** approach to conflict. This is the part of the philosophy that depends most on a trained person in the room. It is also the part most at risk when a program "scales" through content alone.

### 1.4 Evidence over assumption, and defending your choices

The accelerator asks for decisions grounded in stakeholder interviews and usability testing, "not invented personas". Teams present a working prototype on Demo Day and defend their choices to mentors and partners. "Good" is something you show with evidence from the people affected. Good intentions are not enough.

### 1.5 The organization changes too

The curriculum changes with each cohort's experience. BRIDGEGOOD responds to participant feedback "rather than prioritizing what is easiest for the organization" (brief). There is "more than one definition of success": BRIDGEGOOD helps people make informed career decisions rather than pushing them all towards the same job title. The philosophy is a practice that keeps learning, not a fixed doctrine.

### 1.6 What it is not

| It is not | Because |
| --- | --- |
| Charity design ("we design *for* the needy") | The community defines the problem and judges the result. The designers come from overlooked communities. |
| HCD with a social cause attached | HCD asks what users need. BRIDGEGOOD also asks who holds power, who does the work, who is left out, and who grows. |
| A diversity pipeline that only feeds big tech | Success includes civic tech, non-profits, freelance, further study and community work. |
| A self-paced course | It is passed on through relationships: coaches, mentors, alumni, staff and community members. |
| AI-first | AI is "held to the same standard as any tool", and apprentices learn to check its limits rather than assume them away (accelerator). |

## 2. The four terms, made observable

Each term has a working definition, what it is *not*, what each role does or sees when it's present, and signs it's missing.

### 2.1 Social good

**Working definition.** A piece of work is social good when the people it affects helped define the problem and judged the result, when it works for people at the margins of that community, and when the designers who made it grew in confidence, access and opportunity through it. It must also leave no one worse off in ways nobody looked for (unintended harm, manipulation, data taken without consent).

**Not:** a cause area ("we did a project for a food bank"), a vanity count (pages, downloads, hours volunteered), or a feeling of having helped.

| Role | What they do or see when it's present |
| --- | --- |
| Student | Can say, in the community's words, what changed for whom, and show the evidence (interview notes, test findings, partner feedback). Can also say what they themselves can do now that they couldn't before. |
| Mentor | Asks "how do you know?" before "what do you think?". Feedback covers both the work and the student's growth. |
| Ambassador | Picks projects where a partner can actually bring community members in. Turns down projects that are really free labor for a well-funded team. |
| Partner | Writes success measures in its community's terms (time saved, a service people can now reach, trust) and takes part in judging the result. |

**Signs it's missing**
- Success is measured in clicks, sign-ups or "deliverable shipped".
- Nobody from the affected community saw the work before it was called done.
- The student team's names, roles and learning are missing from the case study.
- The project could have been done by any agency, with no change in who decided or who benefited.

### 2.2 Inclusion

**Working definition.** Inclusion means changing **who decides**, not only who is invited. The project names the people most likely to be left out at the start. Those people, or people who share their experience, hold real decision-making roles. The team compares who is in the room with who isn't (reflection prompts 5 and 6).

**Not:** a diverse stock photo, an "everyone welcome" line, or consulting people after the decisions are made.

| Role | What they do or see when it's present |
| --- | --- |
| Student | Names, before researching, which groups the work might miss and how they'll reach them. Treats their own lived experience as evidence, and checks it against others' experience. |
| Mentor | Invites quieter students in first. Shares the room they've been given in their own career, for example by bringing a student into a real critique at their company. |
| Ambassador | Recruits mentors and partners who reflect the communities served. Brings students into rooms where budgets and decisions are made. Arranges payment for community members' time. |
| Partner | Says which groups are hardest to reach. Gives the team access to decision-makers, not only to a website login. |

**Signs it's missing**
- Personas were invented instead of met.
- The same voices speak in every critique.
- Community members are thanked in the credits but had no say in what was built.
- Eligibility or vetting steps quietly screen out the people the program exists for. For example: a portfolio required before you can join a program that exists to help people build one, or vetting that assumes a desktop, a fixed schedule, or fluent English.

### 2.3 Access

**Working definition.** People can perceive, understand, use **and afford** what is designed and the program itself. That covers ability, money, time, devices and connectivity, language and reading level, and networks. WCAG 2.2 AA is the floor, not the goal. BRIDGEGOOD treats access as something the *organization* provides (tuition, food, tools, coaching, introductions), not only a property of an interface.

**Not:** an accessibility audit added at the end, or "free" programs with hidden costs: unpaid time, required equipment, or travel nobody accounted for.

| Role | What they do or see when it's present |
| --- | --- |
| Student | Accessibility is part of the work from week one (the apprenticeship gives it a full week). Tests with disabled people, on low-end phones and slow connections, and in the languages the community uses. Sees exactly what the program covers and what it expects of them. |
| Mentor | Reviews contrast, focus order, reading level and language as part of craft feedback, not as a separate "a11y pass". Notices when a student is missing sessions for reasons that need support, not judgment, and involves staff. |
| Ambassador | Budgets to remove barriers locally (seats, meals, software, transit, childcare) using the sponsor model, rather than copying the curriculum without the support. |
| Partner | Names access needs in the brief: disability, language, literacy, connectivity, age. Includes people who can't use a smartphone at all. |

**Signs it's missing**
- The platform itself assumes a laptop, fast internet, or a reading level above 8th grade. BRIDGEGOOD's own design system targets a 6th to 8th grade reading level.
- "Accessibility" appears only in a final checklist.
- A chapter outside Oakland runs the curriculum without the meals, coaching or software that make attendance possible.
- Students drop out for money or time reasons, and the program never hears why.

### 2.4 Human-centered

**Working definition.** BRIDGEGOOD centers people at **three** levels:
1. **The people the design serves.** This is standard HCD: research, testing, iteration.
2. **The people doing the design.** Their confidence, belonging and career path are outcomes the program designs for.
3. **The relationships that carry the philosophy.** Judgment, encouragement, hard conversations and trust sit with people (coaches, mentors, staff, alumni, community members). Tools support those people. They never stand in for them.

**Not:** a method name, a persona deck, or an AI feature with "human in the loop" in small print.

| Role | What they do or see when it's present |
| --- | --- |
| Student | Always knows which person to go to: mentor, coach, ambassador, or staff for things beyond design. Their reflections belong to them. They choose what to share. |
| Mentor | Spends time on the relationship (office hours, check-ins, career honesty), not only on scoring. Asks how their mentoring is landing. |
| Ambassador | Runs check-ins with the local cohort and passes what they hear back to BRIDGEGOOD. Knows when to hand a situation to BRIDGEGOOD staff. |
| Partner | Has a named person at BRIDGEGOOD or the chapter. An AI draft is never the last step. |

**Signs it's missing**
- The first and last thing a user meets is a form or a chatbot.
- AI output is presented as a verdict on a person or their work.
- Nobody can tell who reviews a submission or a brief.
- A student in crisis has a messaging feature but no route to a trained person.

## 3. Local adaptation, with a loop back to BRIDGEGOOD

The brief asks for two things that pull against each other: communities should interpret the philosophy for themselves, **and** BRIDGEGOOD should keep authorship. This model holds both.

### 3.1 Core commitments vs local practice

**Core commitments.** BRIDGEGOOD owns these and chapters don't change them. Draft list for BRIDGEGOOD to confirm:
1. The affected community helps define the problem and judge the result.
2. Designers from overlooked communities do the work, and their growth counts as an outcome.
3. Barriers to taking part are removed, not just tolerated (cost, time, tools, access needs).
4. Feedback from participants and communities changes plans, even when that's harder.
5. Judgment about people stays with people. Tools support that judgment.
6. Consent comes first: no stories, work or images used without explicit, revocable permission.

**Local practice.** Chapters adapt these openly:
- which communities and focus areas they work with (the accelerator's nine focus areas are a starting menu)
- languages, meeting formats (in person, hybrid), schedules
- how community members are paid for their time
- which local partners, employers and spaces are involved
- examples, case studies and vocabulary that fit the place

### 3.2 How adaptation flows back

1. **Adaptation note.** When a chapter changes how it practices something, it writes a short note: what we changed, why, what we kept, and what happened. This is the unit of feedback, not a survey score.
2. **Project retrospective.** At the end of every capstone, the partner, students, mentor and, where possible, community members each say what worked and what didn't. Only aggregated, anonymized patterns go to BRIDGEGOOD. Named quotes travel only with consent.
3. **BRIDGEGOOD stewards review.** Each note gets one of three outcomes:
   - **stays local**: published on the chapter page as "how Houston practices this"
   - **becomes a recognized variant**: listed beside the core, credited to the chapter
   - **changes the core**: becomes a new version, with a changelog that credits who it came from
4. **Visible versions.** The philosophy carries a version and date, and a public changelog. Every page states that BRIDGEGOOD authors and stewards it, and adaptations credit the chapter. People can see how it evolves and who shapes it, while authorship stays clear.

This turns "feedback changes the plan" from a promise into something people can see: you can trace a line in the philosophy back to a chapter's note.

## 4. What this means for the app

The current build is good on several fronts: principles framed by role, a glossary with "in practice", consent rules in the scoping assistant, and clear synthetic labels. These changes would make the four terms real in the product. *Pages* refers to current routes.

### 4.1 Change

1. **Welcome `/`: state the dual outcome.** The hero line "Design that starts with community, and leaves it stronger" reads as a slogan. Replace it with a concrete statement: who designs, for whom, and what changes for both. Use BRIDGEGOOD's approved mission once supplied. The placeholder blockquote is still there.
2. **Welcome comparison table: add the rows that actually differ.** Add "Who pays to take part, and what barriers are removed?" and "How are identity, discrimination and conflict handled?". These are where BRIDGEGOOD differs most from HCD and pro bono design, and the table leaves them out.
3. **Glossary (`src/content/glossary.ts`): widen "Access".** Today it covers only disability and bandwidth. Add cost, time, tools, language and networks. Add a "What it isn't" line and a "How you'd notice it's missing" line to the four key terms. Add "Evidence over assumption" and "Belonging" as terms. Both are BRIDGEGOOD vocabulary.
4. **Principles vs pillars.** The app has six *principles* (`src/content/principles.ts`). The team spec has five *pillars* (Design for Good, Accessibility & Inclusion, Responsible AI, Craft, Ethics & Impact). The accelerator page has its own (evidence over assumption, ship to defend, and others). Showing all three will confuse people. Proposal: **principles = how BRIDGEGOOD works** (values, for every role), **pillars = what students learn** (curriculum). Show the link between them once, explicitly, and ask BRIDGEGOOD which wording is canonical.
5. **Partner scoping (`src/lib/brief.ts`, `/api/scope`, `/partner/submit`): make community participation part of the brief.** Add these fields:
   - "How will community members take part, and how will they be paid for their time?"
   - "Who decides when it's done?" (community sign-off)
   - "Who might be left out or harmed?"
   - "Assumptions to test with the community"

   The assistant should treat the partner's account of the community as a hypothesis for the team to test, not a finding.
6. **Mentor review (`src/content/seed.ts` RUBRIC, `/mentor/submissions`): growth, not pass/fail.** The team spec's mentor journey ends with "evaluates student success or failure". That clashes with "more than one definition of success". Keep the developmental levels (Emerging to Exemplary). Pair every mentor review with the student's own reflection. Add a growth view over time, aligned with the apprenticeship's Craft, Communication and Initiative. Remove any single-person pass/fail decision.
7. **"Certified Design for Good Designer".** Don't build this yet. Who certifies, against what, and what it signals to employers is BRIDGEGOOD's call. A badge from one mentor's rubric would be the checklist the brief warns about.
8. **Student reflection (`/student/reflection`): prompts at moments, not as a list.** Attach the 12 reflection prompts to moments in the project. "Whose voice hasn't been heard?" fits when planning research. "Easiest for us or most useful for the community?" fits when choosing a direction. Reflections stay private by default, and the student chooses what to share.
9. **A person on every screen.** Every AI surface and every submission shows who reviews it next, plus a "talk to a person" route. The student area needs a clearly separate route to BRIDGEGOOD staff for anything beyond design: conflict, discrimination, life challenges. That keeps the social-worker-informed approach with trained people.
10. **Show what's covered and what's expected (recruitment bonus).** Add a plain "Is this for you?" page. It covers what BRIDGEGOOD provides (tuition-free, software, meals, coaching, support) and what it asks (attendance, openness to feedback, defending your choices), so candidates can see whether they're aligned. This uses public facts, with no invented testimonials.
11. **Ambassador toolkits (`/ambassador/toolkits`): add "Removing barriers locally".** Cover budgeting seats, meals, software and transit through sponsors, so chapters copy the support along with the curriculum.
12. **Chapters (`/chapters`): show local practice.** Each chapter page shows its adaptation notes ("how we practice this here") and links to the core commitments.
13. **Philosophy versioning.** Show a version, date and changelog on the Welcome page, with credit lines for chapter contributions (section 3.2).

### 4.2 Reconsider: desktop-first conflicts with access

The rebuild made the app desktop-first. That suits mentor review queues and ambassador operations. It conflicts with **access** for the people the philosophy is most about: early-career students and staff at under-resourced non-profits are often phone-first or on shared or older devices. BRIDGEGOOD itself points out that a third of people worldwide have no internet access. Proposal: keep the public pages, student area and partner submission phone-first and low-bandwidth. Keep desktop-first layouts for mentor and ambassador work. Also follow the design system's 6th to 8th grade reading level and US spelling. BRIDGEGOOD is an Oakland organization and its pages use US spelling, while app content currently mixes in UK spelling ("organisation", "programme", "centred").

### 4.3 Remove or don't add

- **The "4 roles" stat on Welcome.** It's about our app, not about the community.
- **Unsourced statistics.** Several figures on BRIDGEGOOD's public pages lack clear sources. Don't copy them into the app until BRIDGEGOOD confirms them.
- **Any AI verdict on a person.** No AI scoring of students, eligibility or "fit". The AI evaluator can comment on a *design* against the principles. It must never judge the designer.
- **Identity data collection for vetting.** Vetting for "underrepresented" status should happen in human conversation, run by BRIDGEGOOD or the ambassador. It shouldn't be a form field the app stores.
- **Pass/fail language** anywhere in student flows.
- **"Coming soon" placeholders that look like content** (Welcome Resources). Either show what will be there and who's making it, or leave them out.

### 4.4 Gaps worth naming

- **Alumni are missing as a role.** The brief names alumni explicitly. BRIDGEGOOD alumni come back for open labs, and some coaches become mentors. A student → alumni → coach/mentor path is how the philosophy is passed on through relationships at scale, and it's the most direct answer to "beyond any one person".
- **Community members (the people a capstone serves) have no voice in the app.** They should at least have a lightweight, consent-based way to give feedback and sign off, with help from the partner. It doesn't have to be an account.
- **Staff and volunteers** appear only implicitly, as "BRIDGEGOOD reviews".

## 5. Open questions for BRIDGEGOOD

1. **Canonical language.** Which framing should the app use: the six principles, the five pillars, the accelerator's pillars, or a merged set? Who approves the wording?
2. **Core commitments.** Is the draft list in 3.1 right? What's missing, and what's too strong?
3. **Stewardship.** Who reviews chapter adaptation notes, how often, and how do changes to the core get announced and credited?
4. **Licensing.** On what terms can outside organizations use and adapt the philosophy? For example, an open licence with attribution, plus BRIDGEGOOD keeping the name.
5. **Hard conversations.** How does the social-worker-informed approach work today? Which parts can be described publicly or in mentor training, and which must always involve a trained person?
6. **Certification.** Does BRIDGEGOOD want a "Certified Design for Good Designer" credential? If so, who grants it and on what evidence?
7. **Material access outside Oakland.** How should chapters fund tuition-free seats, meals, software and coaching? Is the $1,250 seat-sponsor model meant to travel?
8. **Paying community participants.** Is there a policy or rate, and who pays: partner, sponsor or chapter?
9. **Who the program serves.** How does BRIDGEGOOD want eligibility described (the apprenticeship names BIPOC designers and nontraditional paths)? What should never be asked in a form?
10. **AI and student work.** Where is AI feedback on student work welcome, and where is it off-limits? Should students be able to switch it off entirely?
11. **Community sign-off.** What does "the community judged the result" look like in today's capstones, and how is it recorded?
12. **Success beyond jobs.** Which outcomes does BRIDGEGOOD track for "more than one definition of success" (civic work, freelance, further study, community leadership), and which can be shown in aggregate?

---

## Top 5 recommendations

1. **Make the dual outcome the center of the app.** Every project and page should show what changed for the community *and* how the designers grew. That is the clearest difference from generic social-impact design and HCD.
2. **Redefine "access" as material, not only digital.** Show what BRIDGEGOOD covers (cost, food, tools, coaching, support). Give ambassadors a way to replicate it locally. Keep student and partner flows phone-first and plain-language.
3. **Give community members decision rights in the flows.** Add community participation, paid time and sign-off to the partner brief and the capstone lifecycle. Treat partner descriptions as hypotheses to test.
4. **Put a named human next to every judgment.** No AI verdicts on people. No pass/fail from one mentor. A separate route to trained BRIDGEGOOD staff for hard conversations. Reflections owned by students.
5. **Build the adaptation loop with visible authorship.** Core commitments plus local practice, adaptation notes from chapters, and a versioned philosophy with a changelog that credits contributors, all stewarded by BRIDGEGOOD. Add alumni as a role so the philosophy keeps being passed on person to person.
